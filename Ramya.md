# **ORGANISED BUT NOT EMPOWERED: BRIDGING THE EMPOWERMENT GAP IN SHGS FOR RURAL WOMEN IN INDIA** 

## **INTRODUCTION**

The principle of inclusivity is the main vision towards Viksit Bharat by 2047 (India as a developed nation) in which the role of women is to be the active participants of the economic and social change. The community based mobilisation of women in grassroots organisations at the heart of the development strategy is the Self Help Groups (SHGs) under the umbrella of Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana – National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM) is the largest project (MoRD, 2025).

Women's empowerment, intra-household gender relations and poverty alleviation was supposed to be achieved through the women's thrift cooperatives and institutionalisation of credit.It has always been believed that the women's thrift cooperatives and institutionalisation of credit would lead to the empowerment of women, eradicating poverty and changing intra-household gender relations. This is the foolish premise of this policy and is the death knell of it. This is clearly reflected in two aspects: huge scale gap and qualitative transformation in the science-based and rigorous micro level assessments. Though, women's productive assets and economic autonomy is lagging under Credit outreach, there are significant increases in the number of women accessing credit and the autonomy in the decision making of women is also enhanced (Jakhar, 2025). The first section examines in detail the “empowerment gap” between the inputs and the outputs of the SHG, such as the process of group formation, forced saving and taking out of the micro-credit, and the outputs such as agency, voice, income autonomy and decision making power. What are the structural barriers (that have long been a part of the credit delivery system) doing to remain in place despite unprecedented state involvement, state capital mobilisation and patriarchy and elite capture?

This analysis not only considers women and rural people as ‘markers' but also tackles systemic and institutional and economic barriers to women's access and control of resources in rural areas. The analysis is based on structural design of DAY-NRLM, their experiences at grassroots level and how the policy interventions will need to be changed to meet the need for empowerment. Finally, in the implementation of the vision – Viksit Bharat at 2047 – there is a need for a complete paradigm shift and of transforming SHGs into strong institutions to build true leaders, enhance the economic strength of rural India and create a real gender parity.

## **PROBLEM IDENTIFICATION AND CONTEXT**

The empirical reality of India's SHG programme reveals a profound policy paradox: while the world's largest community mobilisation of women has achieved unprecedented scale, it has simultaneously generated an 'empowerment gap'—the chasm between organisational connectivity and substantive transformation. This gap manifests through three interwoven dimensions: economic capitalisation failures, entrenched patriarchal agency constraints, and an underdeveloped institutional architecture for collective action.

The DAY-NRLM architecture was predicated on a linear graduation model: from thrift mobilisation to institutional credit, then to enterprise investment and ultimately household income sustainability. Yet field evidence demonstrates this pipeline systematically fractures at its core. National registries identify 5.56 million credit-linked SHGs, but the arithmetic of capital inadequacy is stark: with an average disbursement of ₹3.75 lakh per group, total credit falls dramatically short of the ₹10-15 lakh minimum Kochar et al. (2022) establish as necessary for shifting intra-household bargaining dynamics. Field studies from Hapur district confirm this institutional failure—44% of BPL women accessed loans, yet 92.99% observed no commensurate improvement in income generation or asset accumulation. Compounding this misallocation, the entrenched policy norm of equal division distributes capital indiscriminately across members, ensuring no individual entrepreneur receives threshold capital for market-viable enterprises. Only 50-55% of loan proceeds reach productive activities, with the remainder diverted to consumption smoothing, health emergencies, and high-interest informal debt service. This capital starvation confines women to subsistence micro-enterprises that generate negligible returns, perpetuating rather than disrupting poverty cycles.

True empowerment requires agency—the autonomous capacity to define life goals and mobilise resources. Impact evaluations reveal SHG membership alone generates statistically insignificant improvements in standalone decision-making. While women achieve near-universal meeting participation, male household heads retain de facto veto power over enterprise scale, asset titling, and market mobility. Genuine agency demands institutional scaffolding protecting women's economic autonomy—a framework absent from most SHG contexts. Meaningful agency emerges only within federated Village Organisations (VOs) where Community Investment Fund disbursements exceed ₹1 lakh per SHG, enabling women's decision-making capacity to manifest. Without such collective backing, credit access alone proves impotent against patriarchal control, relegating women to passive recipients rather than agents of transformation.

The empowerment gap widens due to systematic federation failure. Government records show only 4.1 lakh operational Village Organisations and 33,000 Cluster Level Federations against 14 million SHGs—a scale deficit leaving groups isolated as financial entities without secondary or tertiary support. This institutional vacuum excludes grassroots women from economies of scale: bulk procurement, credit enhancement, e-commerce access, and formal market linkages. Without shared warehousing, collective insurance, and mentorship infrastructure, SHG members remain vulnerable to intermediary exploitation. The architecture necessary for women's economic agency has been envisioned but remains unrealised, perpetuating dependence on informal networks that extract value from women's marginalisation.

Kabeer's analytical framework illuminates how these structural failures prevent translation of mobilised resources into meaningful empowerment achievements. While DAY-NRLM excelled at resource mobilisation—harvesting unprecedented savings and credit—these assets remain trapped at the individual SHG level. Without institutional infrastructure to convert resources into genuine agency, the achievement dimension—actual transformation in women's lived realities—remains constrained. This systemic misalignment between organisational scale and substantive empowerment defines the central policy challenge: converting quantitative connectivity into qualitative transformation for rural women.

##  **PROBLEM ANALYSIS**

Though financial inclusion and government resources have grown, the empowerment narrative isn't quite as positive, as Naila Kabeer analyses. Empowerment can thus be seen as a threefold process, of resources (precondition: physical, financial, human, social capital), agency (active choice, negotiation, voice, resistance) and achievements (well-being outcomes, structural life choices) (Kabeer, 1999). Based on the above perspectives the study of the building design of DAY-NRLM reveals that there are some weak areas in the building design which hinder the achievements in long term.

The mismatch of the design of the credit is the main issue in the empowerment gap and the subsequent problem of the lack of capital at the resource level. The credit products of the SHG-Bank Linkage Programme are mainly oriented towards short term micro saving and the smoothing of consumption and not investing assets. They are not able to distinguish subsidies on subsistence credit from enterprise capital for developing enterprises. While the average size of the loan per SHG is definitely too small to enable market-oriented enterprise growth, it also clearly does not enable for significant intra-household bargaining power shifts (Kochar et al., 2022). Women's situation is further complicated by lack of capital forcing them to enter the under-priced and overcrowded micro-entrepreneurship sector (petty retail, small livestock) into the vicious circle of poverty.

One of the other resource constraints is an early communitarian norm and an equalitarian norm that constrains agency on scale. After all, the SHG Movement has resorted to the ‘inefficient' way of ‘Equal-Division' – the amount of institutional bank loan is divided into 15-10 parts, but it is agreeable to the group itself. If the amount of loans is high and numerous members receive loans, then there is a risk that the loan amount given to each member will not be enough to develop their business.

The institution deficit in the form of structural resource deficit with DAY-NRLM. Village Organisations at panchayat level are suggested to be hierarchical organisations (in a hierarchical manner) recommended as focal points and SHGs at block level are recommended as Federations (in a hierarchical manner). Thousands of SHGs are running without any support from the secondary level Federations as per administrative information. Lack of access to crucial services like procurement of raw materials, credit, e-commerce platforms and formal market linkages are among the structural disconnection that breaks the link for the grassroots members with services necessary for transformative livelihood outcomes.

The poor record keeping and lack of training in basic meeting skills by the State Rural Livelihoods Missions (SRLMs) is another constraint on the agency. In most of the training modules trainees learn how to conduct basic meetings, and how to prepare for and collect thrift, but do not learn more advanced financial management, digital skills, regulatory compliance and/or market negotiation skills. This has led to savings of the women, but not to their empowerment in regards to business growth and formalisation in value chains (Choudhari & Rane, 2026).

These structural constraints have important economic and social implications if they are not tackled. There is a lack of optimum returns and limited economic multiplier effect of interest subsidy and community funds for public spending. Rural women continue to be trapped in informal and low productivity livelihoods and so perpetuate intergenerational poverty traps. 

##  **REVIEW OF EXISTING POLICIES AND MEASURES**

Huge infrastructure development and financial inclusion at an unprecedented level (NABARD, 2025\) has been achieved with the implementation of the DAY-NRLM, SHG-Bank Linkage Programme (SHG-BLP) and Lakhpati Didi Initiative. However, there has been a high level of mobilisation skills in these policies, but there is little to be gained from agency building from a structural analysis of the policies. The Ministry of Rural Development (MoRD) has registered over 14million SHGs (100million rural women as DAY-NRLM members) excessively. Agency and asset-building outcomes are not used to define success, but rather success is defined by the number of groups that are created and loans are disbursed. Evaluation processes are far more concerned with membership than governance and delayed release of the Community Investment Fund (CIF) has an impact on the effectiveness of enterprise incubation at the Village Organisation (VO) level.

Since the inception of SHG-BLP, NABARD has provided the rural areas with the finance which has been repaid with a much higher ratio than 90%. However, commercial banks are to some extent risk averse as they view the lending as an activity to be undertaken as a PSL task. This means that the borrowers will have to pay illegal collaterals, loans will be delayed and the average loan amount will be an economically tested figure of ₹3.75 lakh that will tilt the intra-household bargaining power in favor of the lender. The "graduation" target will be impacted by the capital shortage, because women will not be able to shift out of unprofitable micro enterprises and these won't affect their household's income.

Viksit Bharat's vision is ‘Lakhpati Didi Initiative’ that promotes 3 crore women of SHGs with an annual income of ₹1 lakh. The programme is a livelihoods transition towards enterprise and has been faced with these challenges such as low capacity of extension services, unequal training programmes for gender and inappropriate integration of e-commerce. They can only be turned into empowering successes when paired with the complete access to the market linkages, collective procurement platforms and supply-chains. Some existing policies contribute to good results in inclusion, and less in graduation due to lack of institutional support for investment and agency asset building (finance).

##  **PROPOSED SOLUTION/POLICY ALTERNATIVES**

Based on the structural opportunities identified in Sections 3 and 4, three policy windows are selected as truly promising opportunities for closing the gap on empowerment that span a spectrum of innovation and support needs among policy levels.

This alternative is an LEP course that includes a credit component. The method used is based on the concept of ‘ladder of graduation' where two forms of micro-credit financing are provided: investment capital for growth and subsistence financing. In case the business plans are approved, the SHGs could secure loans of ₹10-15 lakh from commercial banks provided risk mitigation guarantees are provided by the commercial banks. Loans are directed directly to the tested enterprises in the market and the amount of the loan is directly proportional to the viability of the enterprise. This is a step-up lending instrument which complements the existing financial systems, is scalable, is politically acceptable and is rooted in the existing governance structure of SHGs. It does not address the issue of a lack of capital resources and governance and institutional gaps which hinder agency transformation. But, more credit does not necessarily lead to empowerment if there are no institutional changes.

The other option is “Federation Enterprise Scaling. This is to accelerate the process of setting-up Village Organisations (VO) and Cluster Level Federations (CLFs) with specific funding and digital platforms to implement the Vision 2047 of Day-NRLM where CLFs become an instrument of collective action and market access. These federations act as bargaining groups which allow SHGs to combine resources, negotiate bulk buying, obtain quality certificates, and have access to ecommerce platforms. Medium investment of the digital infrastructure and technical support has medium-to-high transformational potential. Federations create unthinkable economies for SHGs: shared warehousing lowers storage costs, collective insurance helps to manage risk and combined purchasing helps to get wholesale rates. The administrative capacity constraint and inter departmental coordination requirements are the main concerns. For the time being, marketing coops at the Federation level can attempt to increase their market opportunities by developing brand products and going direct to the consumer.

Another is the Agency-Centric Livelihood Combo where the agency provides the livelihood development and livelihood support services that are pertinent to the beneficiaries. This comprehensive view of empowerment will take a multi-layered approach on the capital side, on the institutional side and on the law level. It covers all five measures of Kabeer and is five complementary measures that build upon Two and Three – Alternatives. First is a dedicated SHG Enterprise fund with an investment amount of ₹15,000 crore, instead of equal investment amount of Rs 15,000 amongst members as is usual.The first is a dedicated SHG Enterprise fund with the investment amount of ₹15,000 crore as compared to the usual fund of Rs 15,000 each amongst the members, based on verified business plans and governance compliance. Second, Federation Completion Mission aids in setting up of active VOs and CLFs in each block by 2028, including Digital market platforms for Collective bargaining and E-commerce integration. Thirdly, using the law instruments due to interest subventions to joint assets reduces intrahousehold bargaining power and encourages credit utilization. Furthermore, reinforcing actions are put in place in governance to define binding decision making rules and audit requirements which are essential for the credit to be effective from the agency perspective.Fourth, mechanisms for devising clear norms of decision making and audit requirements that will ensure the desired effect of increased agency to the credit are introduced under Governance. Fifth, ensuring digital inclusion and specialized skills development will equip women with market-relevant skills and capacities, enabling them to leverage technology to create a business.

The approach is an integrated approach, one step to credit design empowerment. The most comprehensive approach to substantive transformation of women's economic participation with respect to three dimensions (resource allocation, institutional structure and legal empowerment) is alternative Three. The strategy builds on DAY-NRLM's existing infrastructure, and also innovates in six aspects – finance, via the Enterprise Fund; institutions, via completion of federations; law, via joint titling; governance, via enhancing transparency; skills, via digital learning programmes; and monitoring, via tracking of agency indicators rather than credit volumes.

Its implementation needs coordination between the Ministry of Rural Development, NABARD, and State Rural Livelihoods Missions and the use of a task force and data dashboards for tracking metrics of women's agency. The infrastructure project with an estimated cost of ₹15,000 crore, could be funded with public money and IDA (concessional) loans from the World Bank using India's AAA rating, thereby mobilising money for the project that is available at a low cost. Before the federation of NRLM is rolled out across the country before 2028, a pilot programme will be undertaken at some blocks of selected states (Madhya Pradesh, Odisha and Uttar Pradesh) in which federation of NRLM is already operational. State level review committees made up of officials, practitioners and women's rights groups would monitor accountability when implementing.

##  **CONCLUSION**

The SHG programme in India has now gathered a great momentum with millions of women in rural areas forming into SHGs. Financial organisation, however, is not enough to change life as is shown by empowerment gap. Simple fact is that a lack of agency, a lack of governance and a lack of institutional infrastructure is not sufficient to tip the balance in the bargaining power of households and to ensure the sustainable livelihood of household members. Despite this, more than 14 million SHGs have been organized, but the majority of the women are yet to benefit from the economies of scale needed to bring about meaningful economic change as there are no functional Village Organisations and Cluster Level Federations.

Policymakers must move away from emphasizing quantitative indicators and begin to evaluate qualitative results. The three level federation system, from the village level and cluster level federations, is not just a dream, but a necessity for women's economic empowerment. However, if these structures are not in place, SHGs are isolated financial units, which are open to exploitation by local intermediaries.

There are three future reforms that are required: a dedicated Enterprise Fund, credit based on documented business plans; the introduction of digital market platforms and advanced training to speed up the establishment of the federation network; and interest subvention based on joint asset-titling, which will change the intra-household dynamics. The only way this system will be capable of changing can be done if India's organised but disempowered women are agents of their own development and the vision of Viksit Bharat 2047 is inclusive growth.
