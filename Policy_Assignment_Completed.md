### 1. Introduction

India's ambitious journey towards becoming a developed nation by 2047—articulated through the national Viksit Bharat@2047 vision—hinges crucially on inclusive growth, wherein women transition from being passive beneficiaries of welfare to active agents of economic and social transformation. At the heart of this developmental strategy lies the world's largest community-led women's mobilisation effort: Self-Help Groups (SHGs) under the Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana–National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM), which have successfully organised over 100 million rural women into grassroots collectives (MoRD, 2025). For decades, the foundational policy assumption guiding this massive state intervention has been straightforward: organising women into thrift cooperatives and expanding their access to institutional credit will automatically generate substantive empowerment, alleviate poverty, and alter intra-household gender dynamics. 

However, mounting empirical evidence from the field reveals a critical disconnect between quantitative scale and qualitative transformation. As recently demonstrated in rigorous micro-level evaluations, while credit outreach and membership numbers have scaled exponentially, women's independent control over household decisions, productive assets, and economic autonomy frequently lags behind (Jakhar, 2025). NABARD's national SHG-BLP evaluation reveals that while 60% of members report major or total control over SHG loan use after joining (up from 12% before), independent decision-making remains low in many contexts (17% in Bihar), with joint or male-dominated decisions persisting (NABARD, 2023). Loan utilisation studies consistently show 35–45% of credit flows to consumption smoothing, health emergencies, and social expenditures rather than income-generating activities (NIRDPR, 2020). 

This assignment analyses the "empowerment gap"—the divergence between SHG organisational inputs (such as group formation, savings mobilisation, and micro-credit disbursal) and true empowerment outcomes (agency, voice, income autonomy, and decision-making authority). Applying Kabeer's framework of Resources, Agency, and Achievements (Kabeer, 1999), this paper identifies structural causes, reviews existing policy architecture, and proposes three evidence-based policy alternatives calibrated for feasibility within India's federal implementation context.

### 2. Problem Identification and Context

The empirical reality of India's SHG programme reveals a profound policy paradox: while the world's largest community mobilisation of women has achieved unprecedented scale, it has simultaneously generated an 'empowerment gap'—the chasm between organisational connectivity and substantive transformation. This gap manifests through three interwoven dimensions: economic capitalisation failures, entrenched patriarchal agency constraints, and an underdeveloped institutional architecture for collective action.

**Economic Capitalisation Failure**

The DAY-NRLM architecture was predicated on a linear graduation model: from thrift mobilisation to institutional credit, then to enterprise investment and ultimately household income sustainability. Yet field evidence demonstrates this pipeline systematically fractures at its core. National registries identify 5.56 million credit-linked SHGs, but the arithmetic of capital inadequacy is stark: with an average disbursement of ₹3.75 lakh per group, total credit falls dramatically short of the ₹10-15 lakh minimum Kochar et al. (2022) establish as necessary for shifting intra-household bargaining dynamics. Field studies from Hapur district confirm this institutional failure—44% of BPL women accessed loans, yet 92.99% observed no commensurate improvement in income generation or asset accumulation. Compounding this misallocation, the entrenched policy norm of equal division distributes capital indiscriminately across members, ensuring no individual entrepreneur receives threshold capital for market-viable enterprises. Only 50-55% of loan proceeds reach productive activities, with the remainder diverted to consumption smoothing, health emergencies, and high-interest informal debt service. This capital starvation confines women to subsistence micro-enterprises that generate negligible returns, perpetuating rather than disrupting poverty cycles.

**Patriarchal Agency Constraints**

True empowerment requires agency—the autonomous capacity to define life goals and mobilise resources. Impact evaluations reveal SHG membership alone generates statistically insignificant improvements in standalone decision-making. While women achieve near-universal meeting participation, male household heads retain de facto veto power over enterprise scale, asset titling, and market mobility. Genuine agency demands institutional scaffolding protecting women's economic autonomy—a framework absent from most SHG contexts. Meaningful agency emerges only within federated Village Organisations (VOs) where Community Investment Fund disbursements exceed ₹1 lakh per SHG, enabling women's decision-making capacity to manifest. Without such collective backing, credit access alone proves impotent against patriarchal control, relegating women to passive recipients rather than agents of transformation.

**Institutional Architecture Deficit**

The empowerment gap widens due to systematic federation failure. Government records show only 4.1 lakh operational Village Organisations and 33,000 Cluster Level Federations against 14 million SHGs—a scale deficit leaving groups isolated as financial entities without secondary or tertiary support. This institutional vacuum excludes grassroots women from economies of scale: bulk procurement, credit enhancement, e-commerce access, and formal market linkages. Without shared warehousing, collective insurance, and mentorship infrastructure, SHG members remain vulnerable to intermediary exploitation. The architecture necessary for women's economic agency has been envisioned but remains unrealised, perpetuating dependence on informal networks that extract value from women's marginalisation.

Kabeer's analytical framework illuminates how these structural failures prevent translation of mobilised resources into meaningful empowerment achievements. While DAY-NRLM excelled at resource mobilisation—harvesting unprecedented savings and credit—these assets remain trapped at the individual SHG level. Without institutional infrastructure to convert resources into genuine agency, the achievement dimension—actual transformation in women's lived realities—remains constrained. This systemic misalignment between organisational scale and substantive empowerment defines the central policy challenge: converting quantitative connectivity into qualitative transformation for rural women.

### 3. Problem Analysis

To systematically interrogate why the "empowerment gap" persists despite unprecedented state support and financial inclusion metrics, this section applies Naila Kabeer's seminal analytical framework of women's empowerment. Kabeer conceptualises empowerment not as a single static event or input, but as a dynamic process requiring the simultaneous alignment of three interdependent components: Resources (the preconditions, including physical, financial, human, and social capital), Agency (the core operational process of decision-making, negotiation, voice, and resistance), and Achievements (the resulting well-being outcomes and structural life choices) (Kabeer, 1999). 

Applying this tripartite lens to DAY-NRLM and the broader SHG architecture reveals how structural misalignments between Resources and Agency systematically undermine long-term Achievements:

**3.1 Underlying Causes of the Empowerment Gap**
*   **Credit Design Mismatch and Capital Starvation (Resources Deficit):** Financial products delivered through the SHG-Bank Linkage Programme are engineered primarily for micro-savings and short-term consumption smoothing rather than asset creation. They fail to distinguish between subsistence credit and growth-stage enterprise capital. As highlighted in Section 2, the national average loan size of ₹3.75 lakh per SHG provides inadequate capital for market-competitive enterprises (NABARD, 2025). Women are perpetually under-capitalised, forcing them into saturated, low-margin micro-ventures (such as petty retail or basic livestock) that yield negligible returns and fail to alter their financial dependence within the household.
*   **The "Equal-Division" Egalitarian Norm (Agency Constraint):** Rooted in the early communitarian philosophy of the SHG movement, groups routinely divide institutional bank loans equally among all members. While this social norm preserves group cohesion and avoids conflict, it is economically inefficient. Splitting a ₹3 lakh loan equally among 10 to 15 members guarantees that no individual receives sufficient capital to establish a viable enterprise. This practice dilutes public investment into sub-subsistence trickles, effectively transforming micro-credit into an informal social safety net rather than an engine of productive female entrepreneurship.
*   **Federation Deficits and Institutional Isolation (Structural Resource Deficit):** The structural blueprint of DAY-NRLM envisions a three-tier institutional hierarchy: SHGs at the primary level, Village Organisations (VOs) at the panchayat level, and Cluster Level Federations (CLFs) at the block level. However, administrative data indicates a severe structural deficit, with thousands of SHGs operating as isolated financial units unconnected to active VOs or CLFs (MoRD, 2025). Without secondary and tertiary federation scaffolding, grassroots members remain excluded from bulk raw material procurement, institutional credit enhancement, e-commerce onboarding, and formal market linkages, leaving them vulnerable to local intermediaries.
*   **Human Capital and Capacity Gaps (Agency Constraint):** Training modules administered by State Rural Livelihoods Missions (SRLMs) concentrate overwhelmingly on basic meeting procedures, record-keeping, and thrift collection. They lack advanced components on financial management, digital literacy, regulatory compliance, and market negotiations. Consequently, even when women accumulate savings, they lack the technical capability to scale business operations or negotiate effectively within formal value chains (Choudhari & Rane, 2026).

**3.2 Assumption Biases in Current Policy Architecture**
The persistence of the empowerment gap is reinforced by three pervasive policy assumption biases:
1.  *The Linear Empowerment Fallacy:* Implementing agencies operate under the flawed assumption that micro-credit delivery automatically generates personal autonomy. In reality, financial access is merely a resource; without targeted interventions to foster agency, credit can exacerbate female work burdens without granting decision-making control.
2.  *Formal Equivalence Bias:* Evaluation frameworks frequently equate quantitative metrics—such as meeting attendance, savings frequency, and loan repayment rates—with substantive intra-household bargaining power, masking instances where male relatives exercise *de facto* control over loan usage.
3.  *Macro-Aggregation Blindness:* National progress reports rely heavily on aggregate figures (total SHGs formed, cumulative credit disbursed), which obscure profound regional disparities in credit absorption capacity, governance quality, and local socio-cultural constraints.

**3.3 Macro-Consequences of Inaction**
Failing to address these structural bottlenecks carries significant economic and social costs. Public expenditure allocated to interest subventions and community funds yields sub-optimal returns with limited economic multiplier effects. Rural women remain confined to informal, low-productivity livelihoods, perpetuating intergenerational poverty traps. Ultimately, unbridled reliance on credit volume without agency-building restricts female labour force participation (FLFP) and directly impedes the realization of the Viksit Bharat@2047 vision of inclusive, women-led development.

### 4. Review of Existing Policies and Measures

DAY-NRLM, the SHG-Bank Linkage Programme, and the Lakhpati Didi Initiative have established massive infrastructure and achieved historic financial inclusion through unprecedented scale. Yet a rigorous assessment reveals these policies excel at mobilizing quantitative resources while their structural design prevents meaningful agency-building outcomes. The Ministry of Rural Development's DAY-NRLM has registered over 14 million SHGs mobilising 100 million rural women, with success measured primarily by quantitative outputs—groups formed and loans disbursed—rather than agency or asset-building outcomes. Evaluation metrics prioritise membership numbers over governance quality, while delayed Community Investment Fund releases stifle enterprise incubation at the VO level. NABARD's SHG-BLP has channelised substantial credit into rural communities with over 90% repayment rates. Despite this apparent success, commercial banks exercise de facto risk aversion when treating lending as a Priority Sector Lending compliance exercise. Local managers enforce illegal collateral demands, delay approvals, and persist with an average loan size of ₹3.75 lakh—a threshold empirically proven insufficient to shift intra-household bargaining power (Kochar et al., 2022). This capital-starvation undermines the graduation objective, trapping women in subsistence micro-enterprises rather than productive livelihoods.

Aligned with Viksit Bharat@2047, the Lakhpati Didi initiative targets 3 crore SHG women to achieve ₹1 lakh annual household income through SHG-led micro-enterprises. Implementation bottlenecks persist: understaffed extension services, gender-stereotyped training curricula focusing on basic bookkeeping rather than market skills, and weak e-commerce integration limit market access potential. Without comprehensive supply-chain access and value-addition infrastructure, these aspirational targets risk becoming numerical quotas rather than sustainable empowerment outcomes. Existing policies demonstrate remarkable inclusion success but falter at graduation, failing to provide capital-intensive, institutionally supported pathways required to translate financial access into substantive agency and asset ownership.

The systemic failure lies in treating credit delivery as a proxy for empowerment itself, rather than recognizing it as a necessary but insufficient condition for transformative social change. Furthermore, the evaluation methodology requires urgent reform to capture meaningful empowerment metrics beyond simple transaction volumes. Many assessment frameworks conflate financial metrics—such as meeting attendance and savings rates—with substantive agency changes, while ignoring persistent male household control over loan utilisation decisions. This gap perpetuates policy blind spots that allow superficial inclusion gains to mask structural constraints on women's economic autonomy and decision-making power within households. Addressing this requires reorienting evaluation frameworks towards meaningful empowerment outcomes that capture women's agency in defining their own economic trajectories.

### 5. Proposed Solutions / Policy Alternatives

After assessing the structural bottlenecks identified in Sections 3 and 4, three policy approaches emerge as viable pathways to bridge the empowerment gap. Each balances innovation against practicality, with varying degrees of ambition and required support.

**Alternative One: Credit-to-Livelihood Graduation**

This approach restructures micro-credit as a tiered graduation pathway. Rather than providing uniform loans, it would create a Credit-to-Livelihood Fund differentiating between subsistence capital and growth-stage investment. Self-Help Groups with verified business plans could access larger loans of ₹10-15 lakh, supported by risk-mitigation guarantees for banks. The model links loan size directly to business viability, ensuring capital reaches market-demonstrated enterprises. Drawing on existing banking infrastructure, this graduated lending approach is both scalable and politically feasible, leveraging current SHG governance structures. While it addresses the capital constraint, it does little to tackle the governance and institutional deficits that limit agency transformation. More credit alone may not translate into greater empowerment without concurrent institutional reforms and systemic change.

**Alternative Two: Federation Enterprise Scaling**

Building on DAY-NRLM's vision of federations as vehicles for market access and collective action, this approach accelerates the completion of Village Organisations and Cluster Level Federations through dedicated funding and digital platforms. Federations would function as bargaining collectives, pooling SHG resources to negotiate bulk procurement, quality certification, and e-commerce listings. This strategy requires moderate investment in digital infrastructure and technical support, but offers medium-to-high transformational potential. The federation model unlocks economies impossible for individual SHGs to achieve: shared warehousing lowers storage costs, collective insurance spreads risk, and aggregated purchasing power secures wholesale rates. The challenge lies in administrative capacity constraints and the need for coordination across multiple government departments. Federation-level marketing cooperatives could further enhance market reach by handling branded product promotion and direct retail sales.

**Alternative Three: Agency-Centric Livelihood Combo (Recommended)**

This comprehensive approach treats empowerment as a systems challenge requiring simultaneous intervention across capital, institutions, and legal frameworks. It integrates five complementary measures to address all dimensions of Kabeer's framework while building on the strengths of Alternatives One and Two. First, a dedicated ₹15,000 crore SHG Enterprise Fund would provide growth-stage capital specifically conditioned on verified business plans and governance compliance, moving beyond equal-division norms to ensure per-member investment exceeds productive thresholds. Second, a Federation Completion Mission would accelerate the establishment of active Village Organisations and Cluster Level Federations across all blocks by 2028, equipped with digital market platforms and technical infrastructure for collective bargaining and e-commerce integration. Third, linking interest subventions to joint asset titling would create legal instruments that alter intra-household bargaining power while encouraging institutional credit uptake. Fourth, governance strengthening mechanisms would establish transparent decision-making norms and audit requirements to ensure credit translates into genuine agency. Finally, digital inclusion and specialised skill development would equip women with market-relevant capabilities to leverage technology for business growth.

This integrated strategy represents a fundamental shift from credit-centric delivery to empowerment-focused design. Alternative Three offers the most comprehensive pathway to achieve substantive transformation in women's economic lives by tackling resource allocation, institutional architecture, and legal empowerment simultaneously. The approach builds on DAY-NRLM's existing infrastructure while innovating across six dimensions: finance through the Enterprise Fund, institutions through federation completion, law through joint titling, governance through transparency, skills through digital training, and monitoring through agency indicators rather than credit volumes alone.

Implementation will require coordination between the Ministry of Rural Development, NABARD, and State Rural Livelihoods Missions, supported by a task force and data dashboards to track women's agency metrics. The ₹15,000 crore Enterprise Fund could be financed through public resources and World Bank IDA concessional loans, leveraging India's AAA credit rating to mobilise low-cost capital. A phased pilot in selected blocks across Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, and Uttar Pradesh—states with established NRLM federations—would test the intervention before national rollout by 2028. State-level review committees of officials, practitioners, and women's rights organisations would ensure accountability throughout the implementation cycle.

### 6. Conclusion and Recommendations

India's SHG programme has achieved remarkable scale, mobilising millions of rural women into collectives. However, the empowerment gap reveals that financial organisation alone cannot transform lives. The evidence is clear: credit without agency, savings without governance, and scale without institutional infrastructure fail to shift household bargaining power or create sustainable livelihoods. While over 14 million SHGs have been formed, the absence of functional Village Organisations and Cluster Level Federations leaves most women isolated from the economies of scale essential for meaningful economic transformation.

Policymakers must urgently pivot from celebrating quantitative metrics to measuring substantive outcomes. The three-tier federation architecture—encompassing Village Organisations and Cluster Level Federations—is not merely aspirational but essential for women's economic agency. Without these structures, SHGs remain isolated financial units vulnerable to exploitation by local intermediaries.

The path forward requires three interlinked reforms: first, a dedicated Enterprise Fund that conditions credit on verified business plans; second, accelerated completion of the federation network with digital market platforms and advanced training; and third, interest subvention tied to joint asset-titling to reshape intra-household dynamics. Only through such systemic redesign can India convert its organised but disempowered women into agents of their own development, fulfilling the Viksit Bharat@2047 promise of inclusive growth.
