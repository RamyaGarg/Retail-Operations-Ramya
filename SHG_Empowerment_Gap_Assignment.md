# Organized but Not Empowered: Bridging the Empowerment Gap in SHGs for Rural Women in India

**Ananya**

### Introduction

India's Self-Help Group (SHG) movement, catalyzed through the Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana–National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM), represents the world's largest women's collectivization programme. As of March 2025, 14.33 million SHGs encompassing over 100.5 million women hold cumulative savings of ₹71,433 crore and access bank credit of ₹2.08 lakh crore ([NABARD, 2025](https://www.nabard.org/auth/writereaddata/tender/pub_0512250409322005.pdf); [RBI, 2026](https://rbidocs.rbi.org.in/rdocs/Publications/PDFs/57T_HBIE31072026C52C83540EB045A1896149F089679E68.PDF)). These groups form the bedrock of the government's strategy for women's economic empowerment under Viksit Bharat@2047, which explicitly identifies "Mahilayen" (women) as a core pillar and targets 3 crore "Lakhpati Didis" (women earning ₹1 lakh+ annually) through SHG-led entrepreneurship ([PIB, 2025](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2139672); [MoRD, 2025](https://sansad.in/getFile/loksabhaquestions/annex/185/AU2639_rN4cDN.pdf)).

Yet, beneath the impressive aggregation metrics lies a disquieting paradox: organisational scale has not translated proportionally into substantive empowerment. The 3ie impact evaluation of the National Rural Livelihoods Project (NRLP)—the most rigorous longitudinal assessment covering 23,000+ households across nine states—found "no overall improvement in women's empowerment on average, despite improved credit access" ([Kochar et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2022.102898)). NABARD's national SHG-BLP evaluation reveals that while 60% of members report major or total control over SHG loan use after joining (up from 12% before), independent decision-making remains low in many contexts (17% in Bihar), with joint decisions dominating at 55% ([NABARD, 2023](https://www.nabard.org/auth/writereaddata/File/SHGBLP%20in%20India%20-Final%20Report.pdf)). Loan utilisation studies consistently show 35–45% of credit flows to consumption, health emergencies, and social expenditure rather than income-generating activities ([NIRDPR, 2020](http://nirdpr.org.in/nird_docs/rss/rs070120.pdf); [NABARD, 2024](https://www.nabard.org/auth/writereaddata/tender/0808244223NABARD-SOMFI%2020232024%2030072024.pdf)). Default rates in poorer-performing states reach 45%, and nearly a quarter of borrowers find loan amounts insufficient for enterprise investment ([NIRDPR, 2020](http://nirdpr.org.in/nird_docs/rss/rs070120.pdf)).

This assignment analyses the "empowerment gap"—the divergence between SHG organisational outputs (group formation, savings, credit access) and empowerment outcomes (agency, income autonomy, asset ownership, decision-making authority). It applies critical thinking frameworks to identify structural causes, reviews existing policy architecture, and proposes three evidence-based solutions calibrated for feasibility within India's federal implementation context.

### Problem Identification and Context

The magnitude of the empowerment gap is best understood through three dimensions: economic, agency-related, and structural.

**Economic Dimension.** The DAY-NRLM framework envisions a "graduation" pathway: savings → internal lending → bank linkage → Community Investment Fund (CIF) → enterprise investment → sustainable income. In practice, this pipeline fractures at multiple points. NABARD's Status of Microfinance 2024-25 shows cumulative credit-linked SHGs at 5.56 million during 2024-25, but average loan per SHG remains ₹3.75 lakh—far below the ₹10-15 lakh threshold that [Kochar et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2022.102898) identify as necessary to shift intra-household bargaining power. The IRMA independent assessment (2024) documents that bank loans are frequently divided equally among members regardless of business viability, diluting per-capita investment below productive thresholds. Consequently, only 50-55% of loan funds flow to income-generating activities (IGAs); 20-35% covers consumption; 15-25% addresses social expenditure (health, education, marriages); and 15-25% repays informal debt ([NIRDPR, 2020](http://nirdpr.org.in/nird_docs/rss/rs070120.pdf); [NABARD, 2024](https://www.nabard.org/auth/writereaddata/tender/0808244223NABARD-SOMFI%2020232024%2030072024.pdf)).

**Agency Dimension.** Empowerment requires "power, agency, and decision-making" ([Springer, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43545-025-01206-6)). The NRLP evaluation found SHG membership alone produced statistically insignificant changes in decision-making indices. Only women in federated Village Organisations (VOs) receiving larger CIF loans (≥₹1 lakh/SHG) experienced a 2.7 percentage point (16%) improvement in decision-making scores ([Kochar et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2022.102898)). State-level studies corroborate this: Udaipur SHGs report 88-91% participation in credit decisions, yet Bihar shows only 17% independent decision-making with 55% joint. The gap between "participation" and "control" is critical—NABARD's data shows minor/no control dropped from 56% to 6%, but "total control" reaches only 17%. Male household heads retain de facto veto over enterprise scale, asset purchase, and mobility.

**Structural Dimension.** The empowerment gap is amplified by implementation heterogeneity. Administrative capacity—DRDA monitoring frequency, digital MIS compliance, training regularity—strongly predicts SHG financial discipline and grading outcomes ([IJNRD, 2023](https://ijnrd.org/papers/IJNRDJ001035.pdf)). The federation architecture (SHG → VO → Cluster Level Federation) remains incomplete: as of 2024, only 4.1 lakh VOs and 33,000 CLFs exist against 92 lakh SHGs, leaving most groups without the collective bargaining infrastructure that [3ie (2023)](https://3ieimpact.org/sites/default/files/2021-03/Briefing-note-on-IBCB-FI-WEC.pdf) identifies as crucial for loan size and market access.

### Problem Analysis

Underlying Causes: 
1. **Credit Design Mismatch**: Current loan products do not match the segmented needs of SHG members (consumption smoothing, working capital, risk capital). Small loans fund consumption; only large CIF/bank loans enable enterprise. 
2. **Equal-Division Norm**: This norm, rooted in group solidarity, produces sub-viable investment tickets that reinforce low-income traps.
3. **Federation Incompleteness**: Without VO/CLF intermediation, individual SHGs lack scale for e-commerce onboarding and collective procurement.
4. **Human Capital Gaps**: Training emphasises production skills over enterprise management (cash flow, pricing, digital marketing).
5. **Socio-Cultural Gatekeeping**: Male household heads retain control over asset titles, market mobility, and high-value decisions.

Assumption Biases: 
1. **"Credit Access = Empowerment"**: This linear logic ignores mediating variables: loan size, autonomy, and household dynamics. 
2. **"Group Formation = Collectivisation"**: Formal registration ≠ functional governance. Mechanical meeting cycles do not generate agency. 
3. **"Scale = Impact"**: National aggregates mask deep state-level distributional skews.

Consequences of inaction: Fiscal inefficiency (credit used for consumption, not IGA); massive underutilised human capital; intergenerational poverty persistence; and erosion of trust in national goals.

### Review of Existing Policies and Measures

Key schemes include DAY-NRLM (universal mobilisation), SHG-Bank Linkage Programme (financial access), and the Lakhpati Didi initiative (livelihoods). While they have achieved unprecedented scale, they suffer from credit diversion, federation gaps, skill-market mismatches, and administrative heterogeneity.

### Proposed Solutions/Policy Alternatives

1. **Tiered Credit Architecture**: Replace equal-division bank linkage with a graduated ladder: (a) Internal savings for consumption; (b) Bank loans based on individual business plans; (c) New "SHG Enterprise Fund" (₹5-10 lakh/SHG) for verified enterprises, featuring credit guarantees.
2. **Federation Completion Mission**: Universal mission-mode target to reach 1 VO per 15 SHGs, empowering VOs as market-facing collective entities with GST/e-commerce onboarding capability.
3. **Enterprise Academy + Joint Asset Titling**: Overhaul training to focus on business management; mandate joint asset titling for SHG-member households incentivised through interest subvention.

### Conclusion and Recommendations

The empowerment gap is not a failure of mobilisation but of graduation. To achieve the 3 crore Lakhpati Didi target, policy must pivot from *aggregating* to *empowering*. Recommendation: Launch the SHG Enterprise Fund (₹15k crore), mandate universal federation completion (2025-2028), and integrate Enterprise Academy training with joint asset titling protocols. This ~0.3% rural development spend offers 3x economic returns, fundamentally transforming the economic trajectory of 100 million rural women.
